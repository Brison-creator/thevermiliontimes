One Equipment Swap in Arkansas Shut Down America’s Skies. It Happened 225 Miles From Abbeville.

A routine change-out of network gear at a remote FAA radio site in El Dorado, Ark., flooded the nation’s air traffic system Friday. Every flight in the country was held on the ground, and the fallout stretched into Sunday.

By The Vermilion Times · Published by Brison McPhail
Nov. 15, 2026, 9:30 p.m.

It wasn’t a hurricane. It wasn’t a hacker. It was one piece of equipment being swapped out at a quiet radio site in south Arkansas.

On Friday afternoon, the Federal Aviation Administration says, new network gear installed at its radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, helped set off a flood of data that knocked the country’s air traffic system off its feet. Within hours, every flight in the United States was held on the ground. Thousands of planes already in the sky were brought down. Airports from coast to coast filled with stranded travelers, and the cancellations kept coming through Saturday and into Sunday.

El Dorado is about 225 miles north of Abbeville as the crow flies. That’s roughly a five-hour drive up U.S. 167, just over the Louisiana line.

How one box took down the grid

The FAA’s contractor, L3Harris, was at the El Dorado site Friday installing a new router, the box that moves data between the radio site and the rest of the FAA’s network. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said on CBS’s “Face the Nation” Sunday that the phone company was retiring the site’s old copper line, which forced a switch to fiber. “We brought in our routers and did the work, and that’s when we saw the flooding of the network,” he said.

At about 2:30 p.m. Central, the FAA’s network was hit with a thousand connection requests every few seconds, according to the agency. A second site, a radar facility in Amarillo, Texas, is also under investigation. The aging equipment on the other end couldn’t take it. Radio and radar feeds began failing at 17 of the 22 big control centers that guide planes between airports.

The FAA called a nationwide ground stop. Roughly 3,500 planes in the air at the time landed safely, the agency says. Flights didn’t start moving again until about 7:15 p.m. Central, nearly five hours later. The Philadelphia Inquirer reported it was the longest stoppage of its kind in 25 years.

Two days of chaos

Airlines canceled about 12,462 flights on Friday alone, the FAA says. With planes and crews stuck in the wrong cities, the mess rolled into the weekend. In Philadelphia, more than 150 flights were canceled by Saturday afternoon, and dozens more were still being scrubbed on Sunday. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford told reporters that flying should recover fully by Sunday night, though he warned of more delays first.

Duffy said the same kind of switch has been made “thousands of times” without trouble, which is why crews at a remote site like El Dorado did the work in daylight. “We’re going to have to reevaluate now when we do this work,” he said.

What’s next

The FAA says there is no evidence of a cyberattack. As a precaution, it shut off the same function on about 60 similar routers across the country. The FAA and L3Harris are still hunting for the root cause, and Duffy said the FBI is helping.

Duffy called the system a 1980s network running on copper wire. He is asking Congress for $50 billion to rebuild it.
