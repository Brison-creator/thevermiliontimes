We Asked if South Louisiana’s FAA Sites Have the Router Behind the Ground Stop. The FAA Hasn’t Answered.

The agency’s press office did not reply by our Tuesday deadline. Its contractor, L3Harris, sent a statement that doesn’t mention Louisiana. Here is what is on the record and what isn’t.

By The Vermilion Times · Published by Brison McPhail
Nov. 17, 2026, 5:55 p.m.

Four days after one equipment change in Arkansas grounded every flight in the country, the Federal Aviation Administration still hasn’t said whether the radio and radar sites that serve south Louisiana use the same kind of router.

The Vermilion Times put that question to the FAA’s press office on Sunday, along with five others, and asked for an answer by 5 p.m. Tuesday. None came. The only thing the agency sent back was an automatic reply saying the press office is staffed on weekdays.

What we asked

  • Were any FAA sites in Louisiana among the routers the agency changed after the ground stop?
  • Were Houston Center, which handles high-altitude traffic over south Louisiana, or Lafayette’s approach controllers among the facilities that went unstable?
  • Is the fix permanent, or a patch until something else is done?
  • Has this kind of work been stopped or moved to overnight hours?
  • When will the cause be known, and will the findings be public?
  • Does the FAA have a photo of the El Dorado, Ark., radio site we can publish?

What the contractor said

L3Harris, the company that runs the FAA’s network and was working at the El Dorado site on Nov. 13, did answer. In a statement Monday, a company spokesperson said L3Harris is “supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop,” and that more than 70% of the planned move from old copper lines to fiber is finished.

The statement did not address whether any site serving Louisiana has the same router, or whether the company has changed when it does this work.

What the FAA has said in public

The agency’s own question-and-answer page on the ground stop does speak to two of our questions, without naming any state.

  • The routers. The FAA says it turned off the suspect function on the routers where the trouble began, then did the same on “more than 60 other Juniper routers in the system, and we have paused installing them.” It doesn’t say where those routers are.
  • The timing. The El Dorado work was done in the daytime because it was a routine job at a remote site. After Friday, the FAA says, “we will reevaluate when we do replacement/maintenance work.”
  • How far it spread. Seventeen of the FAA’s 22 en route control centers went unstable, along with several approach control facilities. The page doesn’t list them.
  • The cause. The FAA says it has no sign of a cyberattack, and that it and L3Harris are still looking for the root cause.

What that leaves open

Nobody has said on the record whether a router like the one in El Dorado sits at a site that serves Lafayette or the skies over Vermilion Parish, whether those sites were among the ones that faltered, or how long the pause on new installations will last.

We’ll update this story if the FAA answers. [Our first report on the ground stop is here.](/news/faa-ground-stop-el-dorado-arkansas-router/)
